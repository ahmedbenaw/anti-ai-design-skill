Ink under pressure

01

The paper comes first

Everything downstream is decided by the sheet. We put four or five in your hands and let you press a thumbnail into them.

Mould-made cotton rag takes a deep bite and holds it; a crisp laid sheet keeps the type sharp but shows every mistake. We buy in small quantities from two mills, so what is on the shelf in March may not be there in September — which is part of the pleasure, and occasionally part of the problem.

  • StockSomerset 300gsm, Zerkall laid, Colorplan
  • DeckleTorn by hand, four sides
  • Minimum50 sheets
02

Setting the type, backwards

Metal for the small sizes, end-grain wood for anything shouting. Composed by hand, right to left, upside down.

A line of 12pt Caslon takes about four minutes to set and three seconds to drop on the floor. We have four cases of wood letter bought from a closing shop in Margate in 1981, and a sort of permanent argument about whether the ampersand in it is Victorian or merely tired.

You cannot rush a stick of type. You can only rush the mistake. Maeve Garrity, compositor

03

Mixing ink on a slab

Colour is weighed on a kitchen scale and recorded in a notebook that goes back to the first winter.

Rubine red, a knife-tip of black, a smear of transparent white to let the paper breathe through. We will match a swatch, a bridesmaid's dress or a particular shade of wet shingle at low tide, and we will write the recipe down so that the save-the-dates and the orders of service agree with each other eight months apart.

  • SystemHand-mixed oil-based, Pantone reference
  • Colours per runOne at a time, dried between
  • NotebookNo. 11, since 2019
04

Locking up the forme

Type, furniture and quoins go into a steel chase and are tightened until the whole thing can be lifted by one corner without breathing.

Then the fiddling: tissue-thin packing behind the tympan to even the impression, a proof pulled, a letter found standing a hair high, the quoins slackened, the whole argument had again. Makeready is most of the job. The printing is the short bit at the end.

If it lifts, it prints. If it rains type on your boots, start again. Shop rule, painted on the wall
05

The press run

Nine hundred impressions an hour if you feed it well. We feed it at about four hundred, because we are reading every sheet.

The platen closes, the sheet takes the bite, the fingers lift it out. You hear the rhythm before you see it, and when the rhythm changes something is wrong: a sheet picking, the ink going short, a roller running hot. Three hundred of yours will be printed on a Tuesday afternoon with the harbour wind coming through the door.

  • PressHeidelberg platen, 10×15, 1963
  • ImpressionDeep, with a visible bite
  • TurnaroundThree to four weeks
